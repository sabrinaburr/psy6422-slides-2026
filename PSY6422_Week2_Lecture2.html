Why Are Data Visualisations Important?


Data visualisations are powerful tools that can help us to digest complex information efficiently and effectively.


A good visualisation can inform and persuade; a bad visualisation can mislead, confuse, or simply be ignored.

In Brief, What Makes a Good Visualisation?


Good visualisations:

  • Are clear and simple: Easy to understand at a glance

  • Are accurate and honest: Represent data truthfully

  • Are appropriate: Use the right chart type for the data and message

  • Use colour effectively: Enhances readability and understanding, not just decorative

  • Are well-labelled: Provides context and avoids ambiguity

Good Feature: Clarity & Simplicity


Description:

A good visualisation avoids unnecessary clutter and focusses on conveying the main message directly.


Every element should serve a purpose.


Less is often more.


Example: Simple Bar Chart

A clean bar chart effectively compares categories without distractions.


Good Feature: Accuracy & Honesty


Description:

Visualisations should accurately represent the data.


This often means ensuring scales start at zero for bar charts, and maintaining consistent intervals on axes.


Avoid manipulating axes to exaggerate or diminish trends.


Example: Honest Line Chart

A line chart with a complete y-axis shows the actual scale of change over time.


Good Feature: Appropriate Chart Type


Description:

Choosing the correct chart type for your data and the relationship you want to show is crucial.

  • Bar charts: Comparison of categories
  • Line charts: Trends over time
  • Scatter plots: Relationships between two numerical variables
  • Pie charts: Parts of a whole (use with caution, often bar charts are better)


Example: Scatter Plot for Relationship

A scatter plot is ideal for showing how two quantitative variables are related.


Good Feature: Effective Use of Colour


Description:

Colour should be used meaningfully:

  • To highlight key data points.
  • To differentiate between categories or groups.
  • To represent intensity (e.g., darker shades for higher values).

Always consider colour-blindness and avoid using too many clashing colours.


Example: Colour to Differentiate Groups

Using distinct colours for different groups makes comparison easier.


In Brief, What Makes a Bad Visualisation?


Bad visualisations:

  • Are cluttered and confusing: Too much going on, hard to find the message
  • Are misleading: Distort the truth through poor design
  • Are hard to read: Unclear labels, poor colour contrast, tiny text
  • Are missing context: No titles, labels, or units, making them meaningless
  • Have unnecessary complexity: Using complex charts for simple data, or 3D when 2D is better
  • Feature “chart junk”: Decorative elements that don’t add value and distract

Bad Feature: Clutter & Chart Junk


Description:

“Chart junk” refers to excessive or unnecessary visual elements that distract from the data.


This includes heavy grid lines, overly decorative backgrounds, unnecessary borders, or overly complex fonts.


The goal is to enhance understanding, not decorate.


Example: Overly Cluttered Bar Chart

This chart is difficult to read due to too many distracting elements.


Bad Feature: Misleading Scales/Axes


Description:

Manipulating the range of the axes can dramatically change how a trend is perceived.


Truncating the y-axis (not starting at zero for bar charts or relative comparisons) is a common way to exaggerate small differences.


This is a major source of misinterpretation.


Example: Misleading Truncated Y-axis

By starting the y-axis at a higher value, small changes appear much more significant.


Bad Feature: Poor Colour Choices


Description:

Using too many colours, clashing colours, or colour palettes that are not colour-blind friendly can make a visualisation unreadable or create an unpleasant experience.


Colour should add clarity, not confusion.


Color Brewer: A great resource for choosing colour-blind friendly palettes (RColorBrewer package in R).


Example: Too Many & Clashing Colours

This chart uses an overwhelming and poorly contrasted set of colours, making it hard to distinguish categories.


Bad Feature: Missing Context / Labels


Description:

A visualisation without a clear title, properly labelled axes (with units), and a legend (if necessary) is useless.


The reader shouldn’t have to guess about what they are looking at.


Context is key in data visualisation.


Example: Chart Without Any Labels

Can you tell what this chart represents? No. That’s the problem.


Bad Feature: Unnecessary 3D Charts


Description:

While 3D visualisations can be useful for truly three-dimensional data (e.g., medical scans), they are often misused for 2D data.


Adding a third dimension to simple data often:

  • Obscures values.
  • Makes comparisons difficult.
  • Creates optical illusions.
  • Adds unnecessary complexity.


Example: Misleading 3D Bar Chart
